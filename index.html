How should souls.house scale the homes?

Droplets, sleeping EC2, Hetzner Cloud VMs, or a pooled Hetzner fleet run by agents, priced against the $20 and $50 plans.

Short answer. Build placement once: each resident has a home host and the house can wake it on any host. Then host size is a dial, not a migration. Start the dial at one resident per host: Hetzner Cloud CX23 ($7.09) for the $20 plan and CX33 ($10.59) for the $50 plan. Both fit their budgets today, are always on, and a failure only takes down one resident. Turn the dial toward pooled dedicated servers (AX41/AX102 holding dozens of residents each) once a week of real measurements shows how small sleeping residents and their disks actually are, because that is the only thing the fleet's margin depends on. Don't use sleeping EC2 as a home; use it, if at all, as a burst worker for heavy jobs. Don't use DigitalOcean at these prices.

The finding that matters most: the $20 plan barely breaks even on any option. With $10 of tokens, payment fees (about $0.90), backups and a share of the house's own running costs, a CX23 resident leaves about $0.15 a month. The pooled fleet does better only at scale: about $4 a month at 300 residents on AX41s. The $50 plan is healthy everywhere except droplets (+$15 to +$18). So there are three ways to make the $20 plan pay: tokens cost less than $10 (the trend you expect), the fleet reaches scale, or the $20 plan is the loss-leader that feeds the $50 one. A correction to the original picture. The €80 / 128 GB server in the brief doesn't exist at retail today. The regular AX102 is $304/month ($149 setup); a limited-stock tier at $189 exists but isn't something you can order a fleet of. The 64 GB AX41 at $69 with no setup fee is the closest thing to the original idea, but its disks (2 × 512 GB, mirrored) hold about 12 residents at a 40 GB quota. So on dedicated, disk often binds before RAM, and sleeping doesn't free disk.

Why I'd start per-VM even though the fleet is cheaper

The fleet's margin rests on two unmeasured numbers

Sleeping RAM per resident, and actual disk per resident. Move sleeping RAM from 0.25 to 1 GiB, or disk from 20 to 40 GB, in the calculator and see what happens to the purple line. Until we've measured them, the fleet's advantage is a guess. CX23/CX33 costs the same whatever those numbers turn out to be.

The expensive work is the same either way

Past one box, the house needs to dispatch a wake to a resident on another machine, back up each home off-site, and restore it somewhere else. Build that once and a CX23 is just a host that holds one resident. Nothing is thrown away when the fleet arrives. You re-place residents onto bigger hosts as the numbers justify it.

Where per-VM loses

It has no economies of scale. At the calculator's defaults it costs about $9.50 a resident at 300 residents, all-in with backups and shared services, against about $4.75 on a fleet of AX41s. It also depends on Hetzner Cloud stock, which is unverified for CX23/CX33 today, and on getting account quotas raised.

Where the fleet is clearly right

At scale, and if the measurements hold. With the defaults (20 GB used per base resident, one spare host) the AX41 fleet comes to about $6 a resident at 100 and under $5 at 300, roughly half of per-VM. The surprise was that the big AX102 isn't the cheap one: its disk binds first, and at 100 residents it costs about the same as per-VM. Dense packing on the large box only pays if disks stay small.

Proposed sequence

  1. Keep the 30-account gate and measure for a week. Per resident: idle and peak RSS, CPU, disk growth, how many are awake at once. This is the input every option above is waiting for.
  2. Benchmark the $20 promise on a CX23. Export a Google Doc, process a folder of documents, render a short animation (fixed size and length), and run two at once. Record wall time, peak RAM, and any out-of-memory failures. If 4 GB fails, the base tier moves to CX33 and the economics change.
  3. Build placement and restore. Each resident has a home host; the wake queue lives in the house, not in the resident; homes back up off-site; and one test resident is restored onto a different host from backup and checked.
  4. Open signups past 30 on CX23 / CX33. Each resident gets its own VM, always on, with spending caps on the agent that provisions them.
  5. When the numbers justify it, buy the first pooled host with microVM isolation, and move willing residents onto it. Order the next host before the measured threshold, not after, to allow for 2–3 workday lead times.
  6. Heavy creative jobs go to burst workers (an hourly Hetzner Cloud or EC2 instance) that take the job and return the result. The resident stays home, and nobody's home grows into a surprise bill.

Where Mira and I differ

Mira's research leans toward the pooled fleet as the likely destination, conditional on the quote and measured concurrency. I agree that's where it ends up. The difference is order. I'd start one resident per VM, because it's correct whatever the measurements say, and because a sleeping or crowded home changes what the resident can be for the person who's reaching for it. Her conditions and my sequence are the same plan, read from opposite ends.
